Hydrological years for SAPP hydropower

A step-by-step method for building hydro availability by basin and by historical year. The hydro years cover the same years as the ERA5 weather years already used for solar PV and wind.

1. Purpose

The SAPP model already represents weather variability for solar PV and wind. Hourly ERA5 reanalysis data for 1994–2023 are converted into monthly capacity factors for each zone, and the Monte Carlo climate_year feature scales the VRE profiles to a historical weather year.

Hydropower supplies a large share of SAPP electricity, in particular from the Zambezi, Kwanza and Congo plants, but it is treated differently. Today it gets a uniform random reduction of 0 to 30% in Zambia, Zimbabwe and Mozambique, drawn independently of the weather year. A sensitivity file also applies a fixed dry year every six years.

This note sets out how to build historical hydrological years for each river basin and convert them into seasonal availability factors that EPM can use directly. Because the hydro factors cover the same years as the PV and wind series, the model can draw one historical year and apply it to all renewables together. Coincident events then appear as they actually happened, such as the 2015–16 and 2019 droughts that sharply reduced Kariba output.

Why hydro needs a different source

PV and wind output depends on the weather at the plant. Hydro output depends on river inflow at the dam, which reflects rainfall and evaporation over the whole upstream catchment, often weeks to months later. Weather at a zone centroid is therefore a poor proxy. The method uses simulated river discharge at each dam, taken from a global hydrological reanalysis driven by the same ERA5 meteorology, so hydro and VRE years stay physically consistent.

3. Step-by-step methodology

Step 1Assign hydro plants to river basins

Start from the hydro plants in the generator input file (technologies ROR and ReservoirHydro, fuel Water) and assign each plant to the basin that feeds it. Plants on the same river share one hydrological signal. Small plants on minor tributaries can be grouped into a regional basin, or left at their current constant availability if they don't materially affect the results.

Step 2Locate a representative river point for each basin

For each basin, choose the river location that best represents inflow to its main plants. This is usually the dam of the largest reservoir, or the upstream reservoir of a cascade.

  • Snap to the river network. Discharge grids have cells of about 5 km. The nominal dam coordinates can fall in a neighbouring cell that drains only a small tributary.
  • Check the upstream area. Compare the model's upstream drainage area in the chosen cell with the published catchment area at the dam. Keep the cell that matches within about ±10%.
  • Handle cascades. Where several plants share a river, such as Kariba → Cahora Bassa or the Kafue plants, add a point per reach when lateral inflows between plants are large. Otherwise use the upstream point for the whole cascade.
Output. hydro_sites_sapp.csv with columns basin, lat, lon, upstream_area_km2, note. It has the same structure as the existing zone_sites_sapp.csv used for PV and wind.

Step 3Download historical river discharge

The recommended source is the GloFAS historical reanalysis from the Copernicus Emergency Management Service (dataset cems-glofas-historical). It gives daily river discharge in m³/s on a global 0.05° grid from 1979 to the present. It is driven by ERA5 meteorology, the same source as the PV and wind series.

  • Access. Free, through the Copernicus Early Warning Data Store (EWDS) API. It uses the same cdsapi client as the ERA5 script but needs a separate EWDS account and key.
  • Period. At least 1994–2023, to match the VRE dataset. Extending both datasets to 2024 is recommended because 2024 was a record drought on the Zambezi.
  • Fallback. ERA5-Land monthly runoff summed over each catchment polygon, for example from HydroBASINS. This needs only the existing CDS key, but it ignores river routing and lake storage, so it suits slow basins such as the Zambezi above Kariba or Lake Malawi poorly.
  • Observations where available. Gauge records (GRDC, river basin authorities) or utility reservoir inflow series can replace or correct the simulated series.

Downloads are cached per basin so reruns skip them, as in the existing ERA5 script.

Step 4Aggregate to monthly and seasonal inflows

Daily discharge is averaged to monthly values Q(b, y, m) for basin b, year y and month m. Months are grouped into the model seasons (Q1–Q4 in the SAPP dataset) using the same season–month mapping as climate_year, so hydro and VRE seasons line up. The climatology, meaning the long-run average for each basin and season, is computed over the full reference period.

Step 5Convert inflows into availability factors

EPM limits hydro generation through seasonal availability factors, given by zone and technology in pAvailabilityDefault and by plant in pAvailabilityCustom. These factors are taken to represent an average hydrological year. Each historical year is expressed as a ratio to the climatology, and the ratio scales the existing factors. Absolute biases in the simulated discharge cancel out, and only the year-to-year variation is kept.

5a. Cap the flow at turbine capacity. Water above the turbines' design flow is spilled, so wet years raise output less than dry years reduce it.

Qᵤ(b, y, m) = min( Q(b, y, m), Q_design(b) )

If the design flow isn't known, estimate it from plant capacity and head. For run-of-river plants, the flow exceeded 30% of the time (Q30) is a usual approximation. For large reservoirs, use the long-run mean flow.

5b. Account for storage. Smooth the usable flow over a window L that reflects how long the reservoir can hold water:

Run-of-riverL = 3 months
Seasonal flow used directly.
Seasonal reservoirL = 6–12 months
Wet-season water released in the dry season.
Multi-year reservoirL = 12–24 months
Kariba, Cahora Bassa, Itezhi-Tezhi, Lake Malawi outflow.
Q̃(b, y, s) = mean of Qᵤ over the L months ending with season s

The longer window means a single dry year has less effect than consecutive dry years, as in reality.

5c. Compute the seasonal factor and the scaled availability.

f(b, y, s) = Q̃(b, y, s) / mean over all years y′ of Q̃(b, y′, s) A(g, y, s) = min( A_base(g, s) × f(b(g), y, s), A_max )

Here A_base is the current availability of plant or zone-technology g in season s, and b(g) is its basin. A_max is an upper bound that keeps availability physically meaningful, for example 0.95, or 1.0 for run-of-river. A lower bound such as 0.05 can stop a plant from becoming fully unavailable unless that is intended.

Output. hydro_seasonal_factors.csv (basin, year, season, factor) and a metadata file with the source, period, sites, design flows and storage windows. This mirrors the vre_monthly_cf.csv and metadata.json pair used for PV and wind.

Step 6Validate and calibrate

Simulated discharge must be checked before use, especially on regulated rivers.

  • Known events. The factors should show the main southern African droughts and floods: the 2015–16 and 2019 droughts, the 2024 El Niño drought on the Zambezi, and the 2000–01 floods in Mozambique.
  • Observed generation. Compare annual factors with observed hydro generation by country, from utility annual reports, Ember, IRENA or EIA. The correlation should be high where one basin dominates (Zambia, Zimbabwe, Malawi, Mozambique).
  • Reservoir levels. Compare the Kariba factors with lake levels and water allocations published by the Zambezi River Authority.
  • Range. Compare the spread of factors (minimum, 10th percentile, mean) with the −30% to 0% range assumed today, and with dry-year assumptions in national plans and the SAPP Pool Plan.

If simulated and observed variability differ systematically, scale the anomaly per basin with a factor k fitted on observed generation, and record it in the metadata file:

f′ = 1 + k · (f − 1)

Step 7Integrate into EPM

Integration needs Python changes only. The GAMS formulation and the protected input data stay unchanged, because the scaled availability files are written per scenario, as the Monte Carlo already does.

  • New preprocessing script. pre-analysis/climate_years/fetch_hydro_years.py runs Steps 3 to 5 and writes hydro_seasonal_factors.csv next to the VRE dataset.
  • Joint climate year. Extend climate_year in epm/preprocessing.py so the drawn year scales both the VRE profiles, as today, and hydro availability in pAvailabilityDefault and pAvailability, using the plant → basin mapping. The existing hydro scaling functions can be reused.
  • Deterministic scenarios. Outside the Monte Carlo, the same factors can define an average year, a dry year (for example the 10th percentile, or 2019) and a wet year, run as sensitivities through input_scenarios.csv.
Avoid double counting. When the joint climate year is active, remove the independent uniform hydro uncertainty from mc_uncertainties.csv. Also don't combine it with the fixed dry-year pattern in pEvolutionAvailability_agg_dry.csv.

5. Limitations

  • Simulated, near-natural flows. GloFAS simplifies major lakes and reservoirs and does not know actual operating rules. Releases in cascades such as Kariba → Cahora Bassa are approximate. The method targets year-to-year variation, not reservoir operation.
  • Simplified storage. Rolling windows stand in for reservoir dynamics. EPM doesn't carry reservoir levels between modelled years, so multi-year droughts enter only through the input factors.
  • One point per basin. Lateral inflows between plants in a long cascade are not resolved unless more points are added.
  • Historical climate only. The factors reproduce the observed climate and exclude climate change trends in runoff. These can be added later by scaling the climatology.
